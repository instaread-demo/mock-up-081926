Inside Sam Renouf’s PTO Vision to Transform Triathlon

Brit CooperPublished August 18, 2026

Sponsored by
0:00 / 6:41
Instaread

We had the privilege of sitting down with PTO CEO Sam Renouf to chat about all things 2027 T100 Triathlon World Tour – the exciting rebrand and what the new series will look like. But shortly after we started, after I shared my love for the T100 series, having travelled to London, Ibiza, Lake Las Vegas and Dubai in its inaugural year while being coached by Mirinda Carfrae and working toward my pro card…well, Renouf shared that he knew Carfrae and used to train with her back in the day.

We all know triathlon is a small world…but we had to ask more!

And while Renouf rarely, if ever, highlights his own athletic background, we had the opportunity to learn about his days as an elite triathlete under renowned coach Brett Sutton. Renouf describes himself as a ‘college/uni’ athlete – training and racing on the GB squad while completing a degree at Loughborough. As he approached graduation, Renouf shared that Sutton believed he could step up to be winning professional races within perhaps three to four years. The problem? Prize money at the time was often in the realm of $5,000 for a win, and the economics ultimately made pursuing the sport at the highest level non-viable for him.

Why is this important? Well, it perfectly sets Renouf up for the role he holds today. His mission has been not only to grow triathlon on a global scale and make it truly mainstream, like golf, tennis or Formula 1, but also to make the sport financially viable for professionals, solving a gap he experienced firsthand as an athlete and being part of the solution for generations to come.

A Visionary From the Start

Renouf shared that with change and new ideas, there will always be skepticism. And he certainly faced his share of doubt and pushback at the start: The PTO will run out of money. This isn’t going to last. It’s not sustainable.

Those questions were particularly prominent during the inaugural T100 season in 2024, when the series significantly elevated the economics of professional triathlon through athlete contracts, $275,000 race-day prize purses that included $50,000 for each winner, and season-end bonuses tied to the overall standings. But through it all, Renouf’s underlying vision never wavered.

Renouf was a student of other sports, intent on bringing models that had worked elsewhere into triathlon.

“Triathlon has all the ingredients,” he said. “It’s already a global sport, there’s mass participation alongside the professional racing…it has the raw materials to succeed the way other sports like golf or tennis or Formula 1 have.”

“What people sometimes don’t realize is that those sports didn’t start the way they are now,” he added. They too were once fragmented and lacked the mainstream audiences they enjoy today.

By studying the factors that helped transform those sports – including professional athlete representation, the unification of disparate events into a more cohesive, year-long global narrative, and improvements to broadcast and coverage – Renouf became convinced that the same kind of global success could extend to triathlon.

It’s a vision he has held from day one, and one he has never wavered from.

What 2027 Will Look Like (and Where Does Ironman Fit In?)

As many likely already know, 2027 will mark the launch of an expanded and revamped Triathlon World Tour, bringing together the highest-level T100 (100km) and T50 (Olympic/sprint-distance) events, alongside Challenger Series events that will serve as a developmental pathway for elite athletes looking to work their way toward the highest level of the sport.

To this end, the original T100 has joined forces with World Triathlon and acquired the Challenge Family events, which brings 30+ races into the fold – including owned as well as independent licensees like Roth. All of this is aligned with recommendations originating from the July 2025 Deloitte report, which identified the unification of various series and the creation of a clearer narrative and pathway through the sport as essential to its growth.

And this brings us right back to Renouf’s study of other sports. These are the same kinds of structural changes that helped sports like golf evolve beyond fragmented series and events toward a more cohesive, understandable and engaging season-long narrative.

But there’s one obvious question on many athletes’ minds: Where does Ironman fit in?

Renouf addressed it directly, but his answer was perhaps unexpected – at least to us.

“Our competition isn’t Ironman, and we absolutely don’t see them as such,” he said. “Our objective is elevating the sport as a whole…and our comparators are these other sports like tennis and golf. And that is our metric of success. If our organization can be part of what gets triathlon there, that is success for us.”

It’s an interesting distinction. Rather than measuring success by whether the Triathlon World Tour gains ground on another organization within triathlon, Renouf is measuring it by whether triathlon itself gains ground among the world’s major professional sports.

And, indeed, growth in one corner of professional triathlon can help drive growth elsewhere. Many would credit the T100’s increased payouts and creation of a season-long professional series with helping spur the subsequent creation of the Ironman Pro Series, which has brought significant new economic opportunities to athletes competing across the Ironman and Ironman 70.3 distances as well.

Looking Ahead

While the preliminary T100 and T50 events were announced at a press conference held on July 24th in London, further details will be released in Pontevedra, Spain, the same weekend as the WTCS Championship Final.

Having covered this sport for more than 20 years, Triathlon Magazine has witnessed firsthand the significant economic and audience growth that has occurred alongside the rise of the PTO, T100 and Renouf’s leadership.

But perhaps the impact is just as interesting at the grassroots level.

This year, 24 athletes from our local triathlon club took up the sport and raced the Vancouver T100, many for the first time. A world-class event came to their city, and suddenly they had a reason to learn to swim, bike and run.

And maybe that’s the clearest expression of the vision Renouf described to us. Grow the professional sport. Create opportunities for the best athletes in the world to make a viable living from it. Build events and stories that people want to watch and follow. And, in doing so, inspire more people to discover the sport for themselves.

There is still plenty of work ahead before triathlon occupies the same mainstream sporting landscape as tennis, golf or Formula 1. But after speaking with Renouf, one thing is abundantly clear: he still believes it can get there.

Blake Harris and Mathis Beaulieu: Catching Up With Our Project Podium Canadians

We caught up with Blake Harris and Mathis Beaulieu in Park City to talk long-course ambitions, Olympic dreams, and what’s next for two of Canada’s rising stars.

Brit CooperPublished August 14, 2026

T100 Triathlon & World Triathlon

While attending a training and metabolic testing camp in Park City, Utah, the summer home base of Project Podium, I had the privilege of catching up with two of our young Canadian stars.

The first, Blake Harris, has recently made the move from short-course to long-course racing and is already making a name for himself with some impressive results. Excitingly, he’s headed to his first-ever Ironman 70.3 World Championship in just one month’s time on the iconic Nice course (and we can’t wait to be there watching him race in person!).

Mathis Beaulieu, meanwhile, shared his plans for this pivotal LA 2028 Olympic qualification period, which kicked off this May. He’s hoping to – and is on track to – become a potential third man representing Canada on the Olympic stage, which would mark his first appearance at an Olympic Games.

Two Canadians on two different paths, and plenty to be excited about.

Here’s a little more about each athlete’s journey (and why you should follow along, if you aren’t already!).

Blake Harris

Harris grew up playing hockey just outside of Winnipeg but made the switch to triathlon in high school, where he competed frequently in US-based development series races.

“The racing opportunities were just a lot better down in the US,” he shared. “Through that, I got to know a lot of the junior guys, and it’s also where I met Parker.”

So how did he come to be part of Parker Spencer’s squad? We asked because, as head coach of the US team, Spencer coaching Canadians is perhaps…a little unusual.

“Parker’s always held a couple of international spots on his team,” Harris shared. “Right ahead of my high school grad, a couple of older guys were phasing out, so there was space for another international athlete. I raced 17 times that summer after graduating and then immediately moved down to join the Project Podium crew,” he said with a smile, his gratitude for being part of the group evident.

When asked about his favourite part of training with Project Podium, Harris said: “It’s super awesome having a training group of all similar-aged guys. Very uncommon. Everyone’s so tight and everything. We’re always in the best training locations too, alternating between Park City and Tempe.” He added that Tempe is his favourite, as it’s the place that most feels like home base.

While Harris raced short-course for several years, at the end of 2025 he made the leap into long-course racing at Ironman 70.3 Indian Wells-La Quinta, where he placed seventh. The experience was a positive one, and his testing data was also suggesting that his strengths might shine most as the race distance gets longer…so he kept pursuing it into 2026.

“I was really proud of earning a T100 start in San Francisco,” he said, “and of finishing in the top 10 at the North American Champs in Happy Valley.”

He finished an impressive seventh in Happy Valley, while in San Francisco he posted the third-fastest run split of the day: 58:24, or 3:14/km, mere seconds behind Hayden Wilde’s 58:15 and Jason West’s 58:17.

“I also pretty much ran my 5km PR three times in a row in San Francisco,” Harris said with a laugh, noting that his splits across the 100km were only minimally slower. It was yet another indication of what his metabolic testing had been showing – that his forte may, in fact, emerge as the race distance gets longer.

Following these races, he went on to secure his first long-course podium with a runner-up finish in Mt. Temblant, followed by his first Pro Series podium with a third place finish in Boise.

Of course, we had to ask about the full Ironman. Harris definitely sees it in his future, but for now, his full focus is on Nice, and we’ll look forward to seeing him on the Promenade des Anglais!

Mathis Beaulieu

As for Beaulieu, his mind is very strategically focused on how to execute an exceptional Olympic campaign.

“I’m shooting to be the third Canadian, behind Charles and Tyler,” he said. “We need three guys in the top 28 to get three Olympic spots for Canada.”

Beaulieu shared that he has World Cup racing lined up before diving more heavily into World Triathlon Championship Series (WTCS) racing toward the end of the year, all in the name of accumulating valuable Olympic qualification points, with a total of 12 races counting toward qualification across the two-year cycle.

We asked him to break down just how much the different races are worth.

“Winning a World Series [WTCS event] is worth 1,000 points,” he said. “A World Cup is 500.”

But tactically, both series come with different pros and cons when it comes to accumulating the highest possible points total across those 12 races over two years.

Beaulieu is known for his exceptional running, but shared that he’s recently been putting a real focus on his swimming.

“To do well in LA, I need to be able to swim front pack,” he said. “Sometimes I’m swimming three times a day!”

The focus in the water, and also on the bike, is ultimately about setting himself up to arrive at the run as fresh as possible, where his most natural strength can shine.

When asked about his favourite part of training with Project Podium, Beaulieu said: “I think it’s just pushing each other. You might wake up and feel super tired, but you see everybody, and they’re doing it with you.”

He added that Thursday morning track is his favourite – perhaps unsurprising given his running prowess, but also because, in his words, “it’s really hard, but everyone’s going after it together.”

So far, since the qualification period opened, Beaulieu has secured seventh- and fourth-place finishes at the World Cup events in Rio de Janeiro and Asuncion, respectively. Next, he’s headed to the World Triathlon Championship Finals in Pontevedra, Spain, followed by WTCS Abu Dhabi at the end of the year.
